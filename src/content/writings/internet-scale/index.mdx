It's the classic problem that most successful web services/apps will deal with: the number of users on your site has exorbitantly increased that things are starting to get bogged down.

Pages loading slowly, network connections starting to time out and your servers are starting to respond slowly under heavy loads. Congratulations — your web app has hit the roof!

But now what? You need to keep everything online and want the UX to be as fast as possible. Bill Hoffman from Microsoft's Windows SkyDrive Storage Team has a set of three simple principles worth considering upfront while starting to design and build service.

## 1. Resilient to failures

A service may crash or be stopped at any time. There will be network failures. Disks will run out of space. Never let a failure pass silently. Handle all failures gracefully. Expect failures.

## 2. Simple is better

> Simple is better than complex.
> Complex is better than complicated.
> — Tim Peters, The Zen Of Python

Keep things simple. Complexity breeds problems. Simple things are easier to get right.

## 3. Automate everything

People make mistakes. People need to sleep. People forget things. Automated processes are testable, fixable, and thus being extremely much more reliable. Automate whatever and wherever possible.

---

## The Checklist: Application Design & Development

About 80% of operations issues originate in the design and development phase. When systems fail, there is a natural tendency to look first to operations since that is where the problem actually took place.

- Can the service survive failure without human administrative interaction?
- Are failure paths frequently tested?
- Have we documented all conceivable component failure modes and combinations thereof?
- Does our design tolerate these failure modes?
- Are we hosting all users on a single version of the software?
- Can we continue to operate in reduced capacity if services/components you depend on fail?
- Does our design cut code redundancy across services/components?
- Can our pods/clusters of services continue to operate independently of each other?
- Have we avoided single points of failure?

## Operations and Capacity Planning

- Is there a DevOps team that takes shared responsibility for both developing and operating the service?
- Do we always do soft deletes so that we can recover accidentally deleted data?
- Are we tracking resource allocation for every service to understand the correlation between service metrics and underlying infrastructure requirements?
- Do we have a discipline of only making one change at a time?
- Is everything that might need to be configured or tuned in production able to be changed without a code change?

## Conclusion

Reducing operations costs and improving service reliability for a high scale internet service starts with writing the service to be operations-friendly.
